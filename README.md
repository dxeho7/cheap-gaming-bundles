## Not every "bundle" is the same product

Three things get sold under this label, and they solve different problems.

**Tower plus peripherals.** The PC is the product; the keyboard and mouse are the sweetener. This is usually the cheapest way to get a working machine, and it's what you see on most $500–$1,000 listings.

**Full setup bundles.** Tower, monitor, keyboard, mouse, sometimes a headset and speakers, shipped as one box. This version is where the phrase usually comes from, mostly on Walmart and Amazon marketplace listings — and it's also where the spec sheets get ugliest.

**Component bundles.** CPU, motherboard and RAM sold as one package. MSI's Combo Deals are the clearest example: a PRO B850M-A WIFI board with a 1TB SSD for $295.99, or a PRO B850-V EVO WIFI kit with a 1TB SSD and 16GB of DDR5 6000 for $479.99. These only matter if you're assembling the machine yourself, and they're the wrong answer if you want the thing running this weekend.

For most people searching this term, the first or second category is the real target. So that's where the numbers below come from.

## Where cheap bundles hide their cost

> The price on the listing is the smallest part of the decision. The spec sheet is where the money actually goes.

Cheap prebuilts cut corners in a predictable order. In rough priority:

- **8GB of RAM.** Windows 11 plus a browser plus a modern game will push past it. 16GB is the floor worth buying.
- **A GT 1030, GT 1050 or RX 550.** These are display adapters with a gaming label. Fine for esports and older titles, useless for anything from the last few years at high settings.
- **A 500GB SSD.** Two large games and it's full. Storage is the cheapest upgrade you'll make, but budget for it.
- **DDR4 where DDR5 fits the socket.** Not fatal, but it caps your upgrade path.
- **Older sockets.** LGA 1700 is cheaper than LGA 1851, and that's why so many budget builds use it. It just means the CPU upgrade you'd want in three years may not exist.
- **Unbranded power supplies.** The one component that can take the rest of the machine with it.

None of these make a cheap bundle a bad buy. They make it a bundle you should price with upgrades included, because a $589 complete setup with 8GB of RAM is really a $650 setup once you add memory.

## Timing, and why waiting might not help

The usual advice is to wait for Black Friday or Cyber Monday, and there's something to it — the current crop of sub-$1,000 RTX 5060 builds has been discounted heavily during sale windows. But this year there's a counterweight. RAM and SSD pricing has been rising, and that shows up in prebuilt costs within a quarter or two. A 20% seasonal discount on a machine that got 15% more expensive to build is roughly a wash.

The practical version: if you find an RTX 5060 build with 16GB of DDR5 and a 1TB SSD near $800, and it's in stock, that's a good price in the current market. Waiting for a better one has real downside this cycle. Component bundles and bare GPUs are the parts most exposed to memory pricing, so if you're building rather than buying, the calculus is different.
